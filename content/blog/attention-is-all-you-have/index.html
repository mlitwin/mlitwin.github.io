Introduction

Attention Is All You Need showed the fundamental role of a mathematical "attention" in large language models. In using LLMs via developer tools like Claude or Copilot I am seeing human attention as the fundamental limiting factor. You still need the developer to drive the tool, so the tool is best understood as a multiplier of the output of the developer.

Trends and Properties

Software Engineering practice around LLM usage is still rapidly evolving, but some basic properties and asymptotic trends seem to be starting to reveal themselves.

  • AI impact is heterogeneous across different types of tasks. Minor gains, big gains, sometimes worse.
  • Engineer involvement and attention to what the AI is doing is still needed.
  • The amount of attention needed decreases with the power (and thus cost) of the model.
  • Market forces are operative - no strong monopolies (yet).

Economic Forces

What this means here is, I think, that all else being equal:

  • Market forces will tend to drive to an equilibrium between the price people are willing to pay, and the cost it takes to produce.
  • Profit or loss, then, will lie in the deviations from equilibrium.
  • Understanding the deviations from equilibrium will show you where you should be optimizing.

AI Cost and Value

Rough Estimate

Let's do some Fermi Estimation to figure out the value of AI, at least to an order of magnitude (powers of 10), under the assumption of price / cost equilibrium.

  • Software Developers cost around $\$100{,}000 / \text{year}$.
  • Asking around, AI spend limits for developers are around $\$100/\text{month}$
  • $\$100/\text{month} \times 10 = \$1000/\text{year}$ (remember, this is Fermi estimation so years have 10 months), so 1% of cost.
  • AI value increase must be one notch more than 1%.
  • AI makes developers 10% more efficient.

Mathematics

I think the basic principles of supply and demand are operative here

  • No monopoly here, there are several big players (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, etc) as well as lots of little ones
  • Techniques are well known, there are open source models that are within order of magnitude striking distance of the proprietary ones
  • Compute power is available to everyone - sellers of AI tools and consumers alike - so cost drivers like memory price affect everyone; hosting your own won't be much cheaper.

Given this, we will tend towards equilibrium … eventually.

A Mathematical Model

Take $p$ as the fractional improvement of a developer when using an LLM system. $p=0$ is no value gained, $p=0.2$ is a 20% improvement. This is our measure of value.

Let's make a simple model for the cost to the seller, which has a basic scaling law. The idea is it will get harder to make gains the further on we go. Take cost to be

$$ C(p) = \alpha\, p^\beta, \qquad \alpha>0,\ \beta>0 $$
  • $p$ is the fractional improvement in units of "developer".
  • $\beta$ is some kind of universal LLM scaling factor for $p$ - how much it costs to build more powerful AIs. This is our "law of AI nature".
  • $\alpha$ is operational efficiency factor, or more precisely inefficiency - when it's bigger it costs more. This is our factor for the "state of the art" in implementation - we expect improvements here ($\alpha$ goes down).

You will have economic equilibrium when the price, the perceived value, and the cost to produce are all the same.

$$ p^* = \alpha\,(p^*)^\beta $$

and so

$$ \alpha = (p^*)^{1 - \beta} $$ $$ p^* = \alpha^\frac{1}{(1 - \beta)} $$

Where $\beta = 1$, we need to go back to the original $p^* = \alpha\,(p^*)^\beta$, and then we must have

$$ \alpha = 1 $$

Three cases:

$\beta > 1$: Superlinear
Here $1-\beta$ is negative, so as $\alpha$ goes down, $p^*$ goes up.
$\beta = 1$
This is a degenerate case: the cost law is $C(p) = \alpha\,p$, and equilibrium forces $\alpha = 1$, so $C(p) = p$. Seems equivalent to just the cost per hour of developer time without using AI at all.
$0 \lt \beta \lt 1$: Sublinear
Here $1-\beta$ is positive, so as $\alpha$ goes down, $p^*$ goes down too.

What's $\beta$? I don't know. It certainly feels like the attention-based LLM has some power law, pushing towards superlinear. On the other hand, more powerful models can let the developer do multiple disparate things simultaneously, pushing towards sublinear. Theory doesn't tell us, and we (or at least, I) don't have empirical data.

A Word About Economic Equilibrium

The equilibrium we are talking about here is an economic one, rather than some sort of dynamical system expressed in the equations. It hypothesizes tendencies driving together the price of something, its value, and its cost to produce, all else being equal. But if in fact we were ever at that exact equilibrium, that's break even for all parties, and no-one would bother. It suggests a large scale pattern, but that's just to try to help understand where the pattern fails to hold.

What matters is where the equilibrium breaks.

Implications

The basic conclusion here is that market forces are operational, so profit and loss are going to happen at the margins of equilibrium. Considering the various suggestions I've heard, my thought is we should:

  • Look for marginal improvements in process and execution, rather than much more that we are paying for.
  • Distinguish between improvements that market forces will tend to erode, and those that are less vulnerable to erosion.

Erodible Improvements

If any of these become common, the Copilots and Claudes will just adopt them.

  • Generic gateways to choose model
  • Generic token compression tools

Stable Improvements

I wish I had a longer list for these:

  • Bespoke tooling for your specific situation - skills relevant to your team's way of doing things.

Outside the Margins

The heterogeneous effects of AI on software development means we should think in terms of global optimization at the development process level. This is analogous to the discovery of a new algorithm - you need to go back and re-think at a high level how best to use it - it might not just drop in to improve things, you might need a different approach.

Here are a few ideas I've seen or tried:

  • Documentation in code - an implementation that works and is thus explicit, but with the expectation that it will be mutated in substance beyond recognition.
  • Implement breadth first, not depth first. Full end-to-end including tests, deployment. Accept significant iteration and widespread refactoring. This also means few (or even one) developers working on the initial phase of development.
  • More production ready prototypes, concentrating on architectural soundness. Robust e2e harness more important than e2e coverage.
  • Desired developer skill-set is (even) less for specific knowledge of languages and toolchains. This is a relative thing, of course, but a mobile engineer who knows the app store process can be augmented by any skilled programmer without mobile experience.

Eventually market forces would operate on these too, if they take hold, but they seem hard to execute, so probably lots of room before equilibrium.
